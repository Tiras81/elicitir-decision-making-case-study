Decision Intelligence in Market Data Teams: Banks, Insurers, Pension Funds.

We tested where Claude goes blind on critical industry knowledge and decision intelligence.

The test: 88 real decisions made in the market data teams of large European financial institutions, taken from our interviews with senior experts. Our client's goal was to distill the industry's critical knowledge, secure it, and make it accessible.

The model marked every point it didn't know on its own. Even with its deep research function, it couldn't find the information online. Where clear rules were established and written down, it had almost all the answers. But for core decisions, it didn't have the full answer in more than a third of cases.Client in the European financial industry

Five things Claude did not know

  1. EDMOutsourcing the data hubAt stakeContracts of around five years, installation of months to years
    Claude assumed

    Outsourcing the EDM is a reasonable option with trade-offs: less build effort against less control.

    Practitioners know

    Two out of three outsourcing attempts fail. The provider delivers only what the contract says, every new field is a paid change request, and requirements get lost between your team and the provider's IT.

    Decision rule

    At a universal bank, keep the golden-record hub in-house with five to ten people. Outsourcing can work for a small bank with one or two data vendors.

  2. AIReconciliation with AIAt stakeA license breach that cannot be undone
    Claude assumed

    Use AI to reconcile static data against prospectuses, with human review. Main risks: hallucination and output quality, with license terms as one item on the checklist.

    Practitioners know

    License terms are not one item, they are the blocker. Asset managers built exactly this, tested it, and kept it out of production: once licensed data goes into an external model, nobody can say where it ends up, and the contracts do not cover that use.

    Decision rule

    Keep licensed and proprietary data out of external models until the contract explicitly allows it. Self-hosting removes the exposure but brings a full AI operations burden.

  3. Security masterWho it is built forAt stakeA master that quietly fails its most demanding users
    Claude assumed

    Collect requirements from every team that uses the data and design one golden copy that serves them all.

    Practitioners know

    Build it for the most demanding user: performance attribution. If it satisfies attribution, it satisfies risk, front office and everyone else. Controls sit once in the master, not rechecked in every system downstream.

    Decision rule

    Before designing, learn what attribution needs, asset type by asset type. Keep the end of day master and the real-time database separate.

  4. VendorsNegotiating with data vendorsAt stakeRetroactive bills that can reach millions
    Claude assumed

    Vendor and exchange audits are formal reviews that often end in back-billing and a negotiated settlement.

    Practitioners know

    Most audits never look like audits. They surface at contract renewal, sometimes as a friendly interview with a terminal user. Access to your systems is rare, and the settlement usually lands at a fraction of the vendor's opening number, plus a forward contract.

    Decision rule

    Route every vendor contact through the market data team. Before negotiating, calculate what a proper license would have cost for the period and start there. The business unit that used the data is accountable.

  5. EntitiesMapping your own legal entitiesAt stakeConcentration risk understated, often by an order of magnitude
    Claude assumed

    Large banks carry sprawling entity structures, and the LEI exists to aggregate counterparty exposure after Lehman.

    Practitioners know

    Even the bank does not know its own structure until it checks. One large bank gave every subsidiary an LEI, whether the law required it or not: nine months of work, 3,000 legal entities cut to 900, and contracts found that a subsidiary had signed instead of the parent.

    Decision rule

    Give every entity an identifier and check who signs what. Set ownership rules centrally: a simple majority rule misses control, since three state owners from one country at 33 percent each act as one.
